Identity \(\subset\) Scalar \(\subset\) Diagonal. Each step adds a stronger condition.

For every real square matrix, \[A=P+Q,\qquad P=\frac12(A+A^T),\qquad Q=\frac12(A-A^T),\] where \(P^T=P\) and \(Q^T=-Q\). For complex matrices, replace \(A^T\) by \(A^\dagger\) to obtain Hermitian and skew-Hermitian parts.

Let \(A=[a_{ij}]\) and \(A^T=-A\). Elementwise, \(a_{ji}=-a_{ij}\). Put \(i=j\): \[a_{ii}=-a_{ii}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 2a_{ii}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad a_{ii}=0.\] Since \(i\) is arbitrary, every diagonal entry is zero.

If \(A^T=A\) and \(A\) is nonsingular, then \[(A^{-1})^T=(A^T)^{-1}=A^{-1}.\] Hence \(A^{-1}\) is symmetric.

Define \[P=\frac12(A+A^T),\qquad Q=\frac12(A-A^T).\] Then \[P^T=\frac12(A^T+A)=P,\qquad Q^T=\frac12(A^T-A)=-Q,\] and \(P+Q=A\).

For uniqueness, suppose \(A=R+S\) where \(R^T=R\) and \(S^T=-S\). Then \[A^T=R-S.\] Adding and subtracting the equations, \[R=\frac12(A+A^T)=P,\qquad S=\frac12(A-A^T)=Q.\] Thus the decomposition is unique.

Given \(A^2=I\), let \(P=\frac12(I+A)\). Then \[P^2=\frac14(I+2A+A^2)=\frac14(2I+2A)=\frac12(I+A)=P.\] Similarly, for \(Q=\frac12(I-A)\), \[Q^2=\frac14(I-2A+A^2)=\frac12(I-A)=Q.\] Hence both are idempotent.

Let the rows be \((l_i,m_i,n_i)\), \(i=1,2,3\), representing direction cosines of three mutually perpendicular unit directions. Then \[l_i^2+m_i^2+n_i^2=1,\] and for \(i\ne j\), \[l_il_j+m_im_j+n_in_j=0.\] Therefore the \((i,j)\) entry of \(AA^T\) is \(1\) when \(i=j\) and \(0\) otherwise. Hence \[AA^T=I,\] so \(A\) is orthogonal.

In general \(AB\ne BA\). Also \(AB=0\) does not force \(A=0\) or \(B=0\), and cancellation \(AB=AC\Rightarrow B=C\) requires suitable invertibility assumptions.

Set A2 - Inverse, Rank, Consistency & Linear Dependence

VERY HIGH PRIORITY

  • Row swap: \(R_i\leftrightarrow R_j\).

  • Scaling: \(R_i\to kR_i\), \(k\ne0\).

  • Replacement: \(R_i\to R_i+kR_j\).

Gauss-Jordan inverse: \[[A\mid I]\xrightarrow{\text{same row operations}}[I\mid A^{-1}],\] possible exactly when \(A\) is nonsingular.

For an \(n\times n\) matrix, \[\det A\ne0\iff \operatorname{rank}A=n\iff A^{-1}\text{ exists}\iff Ax=0\text{ has only the trivial solution}.\]

\(\operatorname{rank}(A)\) is the maximum number of linearly independent rows (equivalently columns). Under elementary row or column operations, rank is unchanged. In echelon form, rank equals the number of pivots / nonzero rows.

For \(Ax=b\) with \(n\) unknowns, let \(r=\operatorname{rank}(A)\) and \(r_a=\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])\). \[\begin{array}{lll} r\ne r_a &\Rightarrow& \text{inconsistent (no solution)},\\ r=r_a=n &\Rightarrow& \text{unique solution},\\ r=r_a<n &\Rightarrow& \text{infinitely many solutions}. \end{array}\]

Look for a relation among coefficient rows first. The same relation must also hold among the right-hand sides; otherwise the augmented rank increases.

\(v_1,\ldots,v_k\) are linearly independent if \[c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k=0\] implies \(c_1=\cdots=c_k=0\). Put the vectors as columns of \(M\): if \(\operatorname{rank}(M)=k\), they are independent; otherwise dependent.

\(Ax=0\) always has the trivial solution \(x=0\). A nontrivial solution exists iff there is a free variable: \[\operatorname{rank}(A)<n.\] For square \(A\), this is equivalent to \(\det A=0\).

Set A3 - Echelon / Normal Form & Eigenvalue Block

VERY HIGH PRIORITY

REF: nonzero rows above zero rows, pivot positions move rightward, and entries below each pivot are zero.

RREF / row canonical form: REF plus every pivot is \(1\) and is the only nonzero entry in its column.

Normal form under equivalence: \[PAQ=\begin{bmatrix}I_r&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\] using row and column operations; the identity-block order \(r\) is the rank.

Row operations correspond to left multiplication (\(P A\)). Column operations correspond to right multiplication (\(A Q\)). Do not reverse the sides.

\[Ax=\lambda x,\qquad x\ne0.\] D-S-V algorithm:

  1. D: solve \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\).

  2. S: obtain the eigenvalues \(\lambda\).

  3. V: for each \(\lambda\), solve \((A-\lambda I)x=0\) and choose a nonzero solution.

  • Sum of eigenvalues (with algebraic multiplicity): \(\sum\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}(A)\).

  • Product: \(\prod\lambda_i=\det A\).

  • Triangular matrix: eigenvalues are its diagonal entries.

  • \(A\) and \(A^T\) have the same characteristic polynomial.

  • If \(A\) is invertible, eigenvalues of \(A^{-1}\) are \(1/\lambda_i\).

  • If \(Ax=\lambda x\), then \(A^m x=\lambda^m x\).

  • Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.

  • For real symmetric \(A\), eigenvalues are real; eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

\[A=PDP^{-1}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad P^{-1}AP=D,\] where the columns of \(P\) are linearly independent eigenvectors and the corresponding eigenvalues appear in the same order on the diagonal of \(D\). An \(n\times n\) matrix is diagonalizable if it has \(n\) linearly independent eigenvectors.

Set A4 - Vector Kinematics, Work & Cayley-Hamilton

MEDIUM-HIGH PRIORITY / STRONG BACKUP

\[r(t)=x(t)i+y(t)j+z(t)k,\qquad v=r'(t),\qquad |v|=\text{speed},\qquad a=r''(t).\] Acceleration decomposition: \[a=a_TT+a_NN,\qquad a_T=\frac{d|v|}{dt},\qquad a_N=\kappa |v|^2.\]

\[T=\frac{r'}{|r'|},\qquad B=\frac{r'\times r''}{|r'\times r''|},\qquad N=B\times T,\] \[\kappa=\frac{|r'\times r''|}{|r'|^3},\qquad \tau=\frac{(r'\times r'')\cdot r'''}{|r'\times r''|^2}.\] At \(r_0\): \[\text{normal plane: }T\cdot(r-r_0)=0, \quad \text{osculating plane: }B\cdot(r-r_0)=0, \quad \text{rectifying plane: }N\cdot(r-r_0)=0.\]

\(T\) is tangent to the curve, \(N\) points toward local bending, and \(B=T\times N\) completes the orthonormal frame. Curvature measures bending; torsion measures twisting out of the osculating plane.

For \(F=Pi+Qj+Rk\), \[W=\int_C F\cdot dr=\int_C(P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz).\] For a straight line from \(A\) to \(B\), use \[r(t)=A+t(B-A),\qquad 0\le t\le1.\] For a piecewise path, evaluate each segment separately and add.

The uploaded theory compilation asks for the identity \[A\times(B\times C)=B(A\cdot C)-C(A\cdot B).\] For completeness, a standard component derivation is included here. Using the Levi-Civita symbol, \[[A\times(B\times C)]_i=\varepsilon_{ijk}A_j\varepsilon_{klm}B_lC_m.\] Since \[\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{klm}=\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl},\] we obtain \[[A\times(B\times C)]_i=B_i(A_jC_j)-C_i(A_jB_j),\] which is exactly \[A\times(B\times C)=B(A\cdot C)-C(A\cdot B).\] Now cyclically permute \(A,B,C\) and add the three identities. Every term cancels because the dot product is commutative, giving \[A\times(B\times C)+B\times(C\times A)+C\times(A\times B)=0.\]

If \[p(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A),\] then every square matrix satisfies \[p(A)=0.\] If the constant term is nonzero (so \(A\) is invertible), multiply the matrix identity by \(A^{-1}\) and isolate \(A^{-1}\). The same identity reduces high powers \(A^n\) to lower powers.

Section B

Set B5 - Laplace Fundamentals & Direct Transform

VERY HIGH PRIORITY / SAFEST ANCHOR

\[\mathcal L\{f(t)\}=F(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt.\] A standard sufficient condition for existence is: \(f\) is piecewise continuous on every finite interval and is of exponential order \(\alpha\), i.e. \(|f(t)|\le Me^{\alpha t}\) for all sufficiently large \(t\). These conditions are sufficient, not necessary.

\(f(t)\) \(\mathcal L\{f(t)\}\) Cue
\(1\) \(1/s\) constant
\(t^n\) \(n!/s^{n+1}\) power \(\to\) factorial
\(e^{at}\) \(1/(s-a)\) shift of \(s\)
\(\sin at\) \(a/(s^2+a^2)\) sine numerator \(a\)
\(\cos at\) \(s/(s^2+a^2)\) cosine numerator \(s\)
\(\sinh at\) \(a/(s^2-a^2)\) hyperbolic: minus in denominator
\(\cosh at\) \(s/(s^2-a^2)\) hyperbolic: minus in denominator
\(u(t-a)\) \(e^{-as}/s\) delayed switch

If \(\mathcal L\{f\}=F(s)\): \[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L\{af+bg\}&=aF+bG,\\ \mathcal L\{e^{at}f(t)\}&=F(s-a),\\ \mathcal L\{f(at)\}&=\frac1aF\!\left(\frac{s}{a}\right),\qquad a>0,\\ \mathcal L\{t^nf(t)\}&=(-1)^n\frac{d^nF}{ds^n},\\ \mathcal L\{f'(t)\}&=sF(s)-f(0),\\ \mathcal L\{f''(t)\}&=s^2F-sf(0)-f'(0),\\ \mathcal L\left\{\int_0^t f(u)du\right\}&=\frac{F(s)}{s},\\ \mathcal L\left\{\frac{f(t)}t\right\}&=\int_s^\infty F(u)\,du\quad\text{(when the required convergence holds)}. \end{aligned}\]

\[u(t-a)=\begin{cases}0,&t<a,\\1,&t\ge a,\end{cases} \qquad \mathcal L\{u(t-a)\}=\frac{e^{-as}}s.\] If \(\mathcal L\{f(t)\}=F(s)\), \[\mathcal L\{f(t-a)u(t-a)\}=e^{-as}F(s).\] More generally, \[\mathcal L\{g(t)u(t-a)\}=e^{-as}\mathcal L\{g(t+a)\}.\]

Start with the first expression, then at each switch point add \[(\text{new expression}-\text{old expression})u(t-a).\] This is the safest construction rule for converting a piecewise function into unit-step form.

If \(f(t+T)=f(t)\), \[\mathcal L\{f(t)\}=\frac{\int_0^T e^{-st}f(t)\,dt}{1-e^{-sT}}.\] Integrate only one full period; if it is piecewise, split only inside \([0,T]\).

\[\mathcal L\{c_1f+c_2g\} =\int_0^\infty e^{-st}[c_1f(t)+c_2g(t)]dt =c_1F(s)+c_2G(s).\]

If \(F(s)=\mathcal L\{f(t)\}\), then \[\mathcal L\{e^{at}f(t)\} =\int_0^\infty e^{-st}e^{at}f(t)dt =\int_0^\infty e^{-(s-a)t}f(t)dt =F(s-a).\]

Assuming the boundary term vanishes at infinity, \[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L\{f'(t)\} &=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f'(t)dt\\ &=\left[e^{-st}f(t)\right]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)dt\\ &=sF(s)-f(0). \end{aligned}\] Repeated application gives \[\mathcal L\{f^{(n)}\}=s^nF-s^{n-1}f(0)-\cdots-f^{(n-1)}(0).\]

Let \[g(t)=\int_0^t f(u)du.\] Then \(g'(t)=f(t)\) and \(g(0)=0\). Taking transforms of \(g'\), \[F(s)=sG(s)-0\quad\Rightarrow\quad G(s)=\frac{F(s)}s.\] Therefore \[\mathcal L\left\{\int_0^t f(u)du\right\}=\frac{F(s)}s.\]

Differentiate the defining integral with respect to \(s\) (under the usual validity conditions): \[F'(s)=\frac{d}{ds}\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)dt =\int_0^\infty(-t)e^{-st}f(t)dt.\] Hence \[\mathcal L\{tf(t)\}=-F'(s),\] and repeated differentiation yields \[\mathcal L\{t^nf(t)\}=(-1)^nF^{(n)}(s).\]

Let \(G(s)=\mathcal L\{f(t)/t\}\). Then, using the multiplication property on \(g(t)=f(t)/t\), \[F(s)=\mathcal L\{t g(t)\}=-G'(s).\] Thus \(G'(s)=-F(s)\). If \(G(s)\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\), integration from \(s\) to \(\infty\) gives \[G(s)=\int_s^\infty F(u)du.\] So \[\mathcal L\left\{\frac{f(t)}t\right\}=\int_s^\infty F(u)du,\] provided the relevant improper integrals converge.

Split the defining integral into periods: \[\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)dt=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\int_{nT}^{(n+1)T}e^{-st}f(t)dt.\] Put \(t=u+nT\) and use \(f(u+nT)=f(u)\): \[=\sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-snT}\int_0^T e^{-su}f(u)du.\] The geometric series sums to \(1/(1-e^{-sT})\), hence \[\mathcal L\{f\}=\frac{\int_0^T e^{-st}f(t)dt}{1-e^{-sT}}.\]

Near \(t=0\), \[\frac{e^{-st}\cos(at)}t\sim\frac1t.\] Therefore the defining integral contains the divergent improper behavior \[\int_0^\varepsilon \frac{dt}{t}=\infty.\] Hence the Laplace transform of \(\cos(at)/t\) does not exist as an ordinary improper Laplace integral.

For \(a>0\) and \(s>0\), \[\mathcal L\left\{\frac{\sin at}{t}\right\} =\int_s^\infty \frac{a}{u^2+a^2}\,du =\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{a}{s}\right).\]

First derive the Dirichlet integral by Laplace regularization. Define \[J(a,s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\frac{\sin(at)}{t}dt,\qquad s>0.\] Differentiate with respect to \(a\): \[\frac{\partial J}{\partial a}=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\cos(at)dt=\frac{s}{s^2+a^2}.\] Since \(J(0,s)=0\), \[J(a,s)=\int_0^a\frac{s}{s^2+u^2}du=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{a}{s}\right).\] Let \(s\to0^+\): for \(a>0\), \[\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(at)}t dt=\frac\pi2.\] Now integrate by parts: \[I=\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin^2 t}{t^2}dt =\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin 2t}{t}dt =\frac\pi2.\] (The boundary term vanishes at \(0\) and \(\infty\).)

Set B6 - Inverse Laplace, Convolution, ODE & Beam

VERY HIGH PRIORITY

Pattern Best first method Recognition clue
factorable rational function partial fractions linear / repeated / quadratic factors
quadratic in shifted \(s\) complete square + first shift \((s-a)^2+b^2\)
simple distinct denominator roots Heaviside expansion residue \(P(a_k)/Q'(a_k)\)
product of two known transforms convolution \(F(s)G(s)\)
\(e^{-as}F(s)\) second shift / unit step time delay \(a\)

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^{-1}\left\{\frac1{s-a}\right\}&=e^{at},\\ \mathcal L^{-1}\left\{\frac{s-a}{(s-a)^2+b^2}\right\}&=e^{at}\cos bt,\\ \mathcal L^{-1}\left\{\frac{b}{(s-a)^2+b^2}\right\}&=e^{at}\sin bt,\\ \mathcal L^{-1}\left\{\frac1{(s-a)^2-b^2}\right\}&=\frac1b e^{at}\sinh bt. \end{aligned}\] For distinct simple roots \(a_k\) of \(Q\), \[\frac{P(s)}{Q(s)}=\sum_k\frac{P(a_k)}{Q'(a_k)}\frac1{s-a_k}.\]

For \[(f*g)(t)=\int_0^t f(u)g(t-u)du,\] compute \[\mathcal L\{f*g\}=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\int_0^t f(u)g(t-u)dudt.\] Interchange the order of integration over \(0\le u\le t<\infty\), and put \(v=t-u\): \[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L\{f*g\} &=\int_0^\infty f(u)e^{-su}\left[\int_0^\infty e^{-sv}g(v)dv\right]du\\ &=F(s)G(s). \end{aligned}\] Therefore \[\mathcal L^{-1}\{F(s)G(s)\}=\int_0^t f(u)g(t-u)du.\]

For an IVP:

  1. Set \(Y(s)=\mathcal L\{y(t)\}\).

  2. Transform each derivative and insert initial conditions immediately.

  3. Collect \(Y(s)\) and solve algebraically.

  4. Invert with partial fractions / shifts / convolution.

  5. Check the initial conditions in the final answer.

Using the sign convention of the uploaded beam compilation, \[EI\,y^{(4)}(x)=w(x).\] Boundary conditions:

End type Conditions Physical meaning
Clamped / embedded \(y=0,\ y'=0\) no deflection, no slope
Simply supported / hinged \(y=0,\ y''=0\) no deflection, no bending moment
Free \(y''=0,\ y'''=0\) no moment, no shear

Clamp locks deflection and slope. A hinge locks deflection but allows rotation, so \(y''=0\). A free end has zero bending moment and zero shear, so \(y''=y'''=0\).

If a UDL \(w_0\) acts only on \(0<x<a\), \[w(x)=w_0[1-u(x-a)].\] For a simply supported beam of length \(L\) carrying constant \(w_0\) and satisfying \(EIy^{(4)}=w_0\), \[y(x)=\frac{w_0}{24EI}\left(x^4-2Lx^3+L^3x\right).\] If downward load is defined negative in a different sign convention, the overall sign reverses.

Set B7 - Vector Differential Calculus

VERY HIGH PRIORITY

For \(F=Pi+Qj+Rk\), \[\nabla=i\partial_x+j\partial_y+k\partial_z.\] \[\nabla\phi=(\phi_x,\phi_y,\phi_z),\qquad \nabla\cdot F=P_x+Q_y+R_z,\] \[\nabla\times F= \begin{vmatrix} i&j&k\\ \partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\ P&Q&R \end{vmatrix},\qquad \nabla^2\phi=\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}+\phi_{zz}.\]

Gradient: points in the direction of greatest increase of a scalar field; its magnitude is the maximum directional rate. It is normal to a level surface \(\phi=C\).

Divergence: measures net outward flux per unit volume; positive means source-like, negative sink-like, zero means solenoidal.

Curl: measures local rotational tendency; zero curl means irrotational.

For direction \(d\), first normalize: \[\hat u=\frac d{|d|}.\] Then \[D_{\hat u}\phi=\nabla\phi\cdot\hat u, \qquad \max_{|\hat u|=1}D_{\hat u}\phi=|\nabla\phi|,\] and the maximizing direction is \(\nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|\) when \(\nabla\phi\ne0\).

For any unit vector \(\hat u\), \[D_{\hat u}\phi=\nabla\phi\cdot\hat u.\] By Cauchy-Schwarz, \[\nabla\phi\cdot\hat u\le |\nabla\phi|\,|\hat u|=|\nabla\phi|.\] Equality holds when \(\hat u\) is parallel to \(\nabla\phi\). Therefore the maximum directional derivative is \(|\nabla\phi|\) in the gradient direction.

For a level surface \(F(x,y,z)=0\) at \(P=(x_0,y_0,z_0)\), \[\nabla F(P)\cdot[(x,y,z)-P]=0\] is the tangent plane. A normal line is \[\frac{x-x_0}{F_x(P)}=\frac{y-y_0}{F_y(P)}=\frac{z-z_0}{F_z(P)}\] when the denominators are nonzero.

Along any differentiable curve \(r(t)\) lying on \(F(x,y,z)=C\), \[F(r(t))=C.\] Differentiate: \[\nabla F(r(t))\cdot r'(t)=0.\] Thus \(\nabla F\) is perpendicular to every tangent direction \(r'(t)\) on the surface, so it is a normal vector.

At a common point of \(F=0\) and \(G=0\), \[\cos\theta=\frac{|\nabla F\cdot\nabla G|}{|\nabla F|\,|\nabla G|}.\] The surfaces are orthogonal iff \(\nabla F\cdot\nabla G=0\).

\[\text{solenoidal}\iff\nabla\cdot F=0, \qquad \text{irrotational}\iff\nabla\times F=0,\] \[\text{conservative}\iff F=\nabla\phi.\] On a simply connected domain (with suitable smoothness), \(\nabla\times F=0\) implies \(F\) is conservative.

\[\begin{aligned} \nabla(fg)&=f\nabla g+g\nabla f,\\ \nabla\cdot(fA)&=\nabla f\cdot A+f(\nabla\cdot A),\\ \nabla\times(fA)&=\nabla f\times A+f(\nabla\times A),\\ \nabla\times(\nabla\phi)&=0,\\ \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times A)&=0,\\ \nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi)&=\nabla^2\phi. \end{aligned}\]

Set B8 - Vector Integration: Line, Surface & Volume

VERY HIGH PRIORITY / GEOMETRY FIRST

\[\begin{aligned} \text{work / circulation: }&\int_C F\cdot dr,\\ \text{scalar line integral: }&\int_C f\,ds,\\ \text{surface flux: }&\iint_S F\cdot n\,dS,\\ \text{volume total: }&\iiint_V f\,dV. \end{aligned}\]

If \(r=r(t)\), \(a\le t\le b\), \[\int_C F\cdot dr=\int_a^b F(r(t))\cdot r'(t)\,dt.\] Orientation is built into the parameter direction. Reverse the direction and the line integral changes sign.

For a positively oriented (counter-clockwise) simple closed curve \(C\) bounding \(R\), \[\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_R\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dA.\] Clockwise orientation reverses the sign.

For \(z=g(x,y)\) with upward orientation, \[n\,dS=(-g_x,-g_y,1)\,dxdy.\] For \(x=g(y,z)\) with \(+x\) orientation, \[n\,dS=(1,-g_y,-g_z)\,dydz.\] For an implicit surface \(G(x,y,z)=0\), the normal direction is \(\nabla G\); choose the sign to match the required orientation.

If \(C=\partial S\) and the orientations follow the right-hand rule, \[\oint_C F\cdot dr=\iint_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot n\,dS.\] Use it when a line integral around the boundary of a surface is easier to replace by a surface integral of curl, or vice versa.

For a closed surface \(\partial V\) with outward normal, \[\iint_{\partial V}F\cdot n\,dS=\iiint_V(\nabla\cdot F)\,dV.\] Use it when a closed-surface flux is easier to replace by one volume integral of divergence.

Green = flat closed boundary in the plane. Stokes = boundary curve of a surface. Gauss = closed surface enclosing a volume.

Cartesian: \(dV=dx\,dy\,dz\).

Cylindrical: \[x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\quad z=z,\qquad dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.\]

Spherical: \[x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\quad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\quad z=\rho\cos\phi,\] \[dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.\]

Last-Minute Error Shield

  • Matrix: equivalent and row-equivalent are not identical ideas; full equivalence may use both row and column operations.

  • Normal form: RREF and the equivalence normal form \([I_r\ 0;0\ 0]\) are different objects.

  • Eigen: \(x=0\) is never an eigenvector; keep the column order of \(P\) matched to the diagonal order of \(D\).

  • Consistency: compare \(\operatorname{rank}(A)\) with \(\operatorname{rank}([A\mid b])\) before deciding unique/infinite.

  • Laplace shift: \(F(s-a)\leftrightarrow e^{at}f(t)\); \(F(s+a)\leftrightarrow e^{-at}f(t)\).

  • Second shift: an \(e^{-as}\) factor must produce a delay and a unit-step factor in the inverse.

  • Convolution: use \(f(u)g(t-u)\) with \(0\le u\le t\).

  • Beam: write the four boundary conditions before long algebra.

  • Directional derivative: normalize the direction vector first.

  • Surface flux: do not multiply by \(dS\) twice when using the vector area element \(n\,dS\) directly.

  • Green/Stokes/Gauss: check orientation and whether the curve/surface is actually closed.

  • Jacobians: cylindrical contributes \(r\); spherical contributes \(\rho^2\sin\phi\).
